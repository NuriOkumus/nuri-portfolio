Building a department its own deployment platform

One client, one live engagement, three capabilities I own end to end.

The problem

Students had nowhere to deploy their coursework. Everyone solved it alone, reaching for whatever external service they could find, and some were paying out of pocket for it. The department owned no infrastructure of its own.

What I did

Nobody asked me to do this. A new DevOps lab was being set up, so I went to my professors and proposed that this be its backbone. They agreed on the spot. I built a five-node K3s cluster — one control plane, four workers — and the delivery pipeline on top of it.

Capability 1 — Cluster operations

Five nodes, running continuously since June 2026. I am the one who gets called when something breaks. The failure worth describing: Overleaf, which the faculty actually uses, sat in CrashLoopBackOff for two days. The MongoDB pod showed Running and Ready the whole time, which is what made it hard — the obvious check said everything was fine. The real cause was that the MongoDB pod had restarted and taken a new IP, while the replica set config persisted on disk with the old one. mongod could not find itself in its own replica set, so no primary was ever elected, so Overleaf could not open a transaction, so it crashed on startup. The readiness probe was only pinging the port, not checking replica set state — so it reported healthy on a database that could not serve a write.

The permanent fix was to make the probe check what the application actually depends on, and to verify it by deleting the pod on purpose to confirm the repair survives an IP change.

Capability 2 — GitOps delivery

A student pushes code to Gitea and writes their manifests. ArgoCD watches the repo and applies them to the cluster. If an image needs building, Gitea Actions builds it and pushes it to Zot, the in-cluster OCI registry. Nothing is applied to the cluster by hand — the repo is the source of truth, and anything that drifts from it gets reconciled back.

Capability 3 — Networking and mail

DNS, ingress, certificates, egress and SMTP, all inside a university network I do not control. Traefik terminates everything at the edge and routes internally; cert-manager handles certificates.

The hard part was not technical. Every step was a permission and a conversation: node IPs pinned, ports opened for external access, an exemption from the campus captive portal so a 24/7 service is not logged out by a periodic re-authentication. On my own server this would have taken a fraction of the time. Here it took a written request for each one — which is also the job, if the infrastructure already belongs to someone else.

What came of it

The system is up and the full flow has been tested end to end. Students have not used it yet, because the term has not started. Its first real workload is the department’s own website, live on the cluster right now.

What I’d do differently

Settle the network and access permissions first. Leaving them until later meant every technical step ended in a wait.

If something in your infrastructure is stuck, tell me what it is.
